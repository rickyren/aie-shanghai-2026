## How a voice agent listens

Before we see how it impacts our voice agent, we need to look at how a voice agent works. Meet LaoShi, our teacher and the voice agent within our app. She listens to our users, she thinks about how to respond, and then she speaks the response back to the user. In this talk, we'll just be focusing on the listening part. [seg 1056–1061]

So what happens when a user starts speaking? The audio is first passed to a voice activity detector, which chunks the audio into segments based on the pauses the user takes. The audio segments are then passed to the STT model, which transcribes the audio into text and passes it further down the agent pipeline. [seg 1062–1065]

Now, a question to think about: if your transcript contains text that no one said, which of these components should you blame? It should be the STT model, right? And how do you fix the errors in your transcript? You pick a model which shows the lowest error rate. That's what we did: we looked at the benchmarks to find the model with the lowest error rate. [seg 1066–1070]

## Benchmarks vs. our own audio

But this is what we saw. All these models showed very low error rates, ranging from 2 to 7%. But when we ran the same models on our own audio datasets, they showed error rates 4 to 20 times worse than what the benchmarks were telling us. So why was there a discrepancy? Just so you know, the numbers here come from the FLEURS benchmark, which is one of the standards for STT models. [seg 1071–1076]

There are two key differences. First, FLEURS uses audio from native speakers who read prepared sentences aloud, whereas our speakers are non-native speakers who think out loud while they're speaking. Second, FLEURS passes in the entire audio at once, whereas our audio flows through the pipeline I showed you, where the VAD chunks it into segments before feeding it into the STT models. [seg 1077–1082]

So which factor was causing the discrepancy — was it our speakers, or the way we feed the audio to our models? To answer this question, we did a few more things. We kept the same speakers and the same models, and this time fed the entire audio to the models. Any guesses on the results? The results agreed with what the benchmarks were telling us: all these models showed a sharp increase in accuracy, and none of them produced any foreign script. So we concluded that nothing was wrong with our speakers. [seg 1083–1089]

We did another thing. We revisited the audio clip that I played at the start today and passed it through our live pipeline, where the VAD chunks it into segments. As you can see from the audio waveform, because the speaker was not very fluent and took a lot of pauses, the VAD chunked it into very short segments. Now, what happens when you feed these very short segments to different STT models? You get transcriptions in different languages. [seg 1090–1096]

Let's pick one of these segments and talk about it — say the segment where the speaker said "Ho"; I hope that was pronounced correctly. Imagine I just play this segment in front of you without telling you the language, or giving you any context about what the speaker was talking about. Would you be able to predict what the speaker was saying? In fact, all these different words the STT models predicted in different languages sound very similar to what the speaker said in this segment. So is the STT model really at fault here? It seems like language and the size of the audio segments — which means the context we are feeding to the models — are two key settings that affect the models. [seg 1097–1104]

## Why language and context matter

But why should these two settings matter at all? This is what the theory tells us. STT models are made of two components: the encoder, which listens to our audio samples, and the decoder. The decoder is a language model that transcribes text token by token, and the first token it transcribes is the language. So for a very short audio segment where the language guess is incorrect, all the tokens following it will be predicted in the wrongly guessed language. Second, every token the decoder predicts depends on the previous tokens it predicted and the audio segment it has access to. So if you feed the decoder a bigger audio segment, it has access to more tokens and hence more context, and it should be able to transcribe the audio segment accurately. So the theory tells us that these two settings directly affect the accuracy of these models. [seg 1105–1112]

Let's see them in action. We varied the size of the audio segments in an audio clip to see how it affects the accuracy of these models, ranging from live segments all the way to the entire audio recording at once. And this is what we saw: accuracy jumped from 62% for live segments to 82% for whole recordings. That is a big 20% jump. [seg 1113–1118]

But we cannot wait for the entire audio recording before transcribing it, right? Because then our conversation is no longer live. So how else can you increase accuracy? Here comes the language part. When you tell the models the language they are supposed to transcribe in, you can regain 11% of that 20% gap. What is the remaining 9% gap? That gap is context. [seg 1119–1125]

And we don't need to feed the entire context to these models. If you look at the shaded region in this graph, where audio segment sizes range from 2 to 8 seconds, we still see a sharp increase in accuracy for the STT models. So how do we land within this shaded region to gain higher accuracy? Remember the voice activity detector from before? If you tune it so that it waits for a longer pause before cutting the audio segment, instead of cutting at the first short pause, you'll accumulate bigger audio segments and hence provide more context to the models to transcribe your audio better. [seg 1126–1131]

If you think about it, that is how we humans function as well. If you miss a word in a noisy room, you fill it in with the rest of the sentence that you heard. And that is how STT models behave as well. [seg 1132–1134]

## Takeaways

If I simplify the talk into key things you can take away: run your models on your own users' audio, tell them the language they are supposed to transcribe in, and let them hear more of the audio.
