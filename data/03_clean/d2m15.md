## Challenge 1: different hardware priorities

I want to start with some challenges we face in hardware development these days. One is different hardware priorities. Robots are also considered products, and products serve different purposes. Traditionally there are two familiar purposes. [seg 2811–2816]

One is industrial robots. They are built for precision, for repeatability, for durability, and for well-defined tasks such as production lines. So their mechanical design is highly biased toward stiffness, and that results in, for example, a relatively low payload-to-arm-mass ratio. If you consider the KUKA arm that is widely used in the research community, the payload-to-arm-mass ratio is normally in the range of 0.3 to 0.5, which is relatively not very high compared to the humanoid arms we use these days. [seg 2817–2827]

The other purpose for robot hardware is research platforms. For a research platform, the purpose is mainly breakthroughs — enabling capabilities that were very, very difficult or actually impossible before. One example would be the humanoid robot built by NASA back in 2016. It was reported that the cost was around 2 million US dollars, which is quite high. Robot costs these days have dropped significantly due to the fast-booming field, but the assumption still holds that research platforms prioritize research breakthroughs instead of robustness and cost. [seg 2828–2843]

For our case, for general-purpose robots, it's a bit of both worlds. On one side, we do need to enable new capabilities so that we can unlock new applications and deployments. On the other side, we need to care a lot about the durability and the cost of the robot, so that we can deploy robots in the real world. [seg 2844–2847]

## Challenge 2: model iteration speed vs. hardware iteration speed

Another challenge is the mismatch between model iteration speed and hardware iteration speed. This is similar to the challenge we have between hardware and software in general. But for traditional products, even if hardware and software develop at a different pace, we normally have very well-defined product requirements. So if a hardware team and a software team cannot be aligned on iteration speed, they can still align on the product requirements, even before the project starts. This is more challenging for general-purpose robots, because it's really hard to define long-lasting, unchanging requirements when we are aiming for so many different applications. [seg 2848–2859]

For example, if we assume we have a weekly updated model over 24 weeks, that means we have at least 24 opportunities to change the model setup and try new ideas. But at the same time, we can only afford one or two major hardware iterations. So if the model team has a brilliant idea, validates it in week 12, and wants a hardware feature change, that's kind of impossible. [seg 2860–2867]

## Our answer: predictable hardware

So the solution for DYNA is to have a hardware requirement definition that is clear enough and still relevant across years of model iteration. At DYNA, the answer is predictable hardware design. By predictability, I mean two aspects. One is that we want the hardware platform to behave in ways that models can predict, alongside robustness and cost. The other is that we want the hardware platform to be a stable interface, so that fast-evolving models can reuse this physical platform as they evolve. [seg 2868–2876]

For hardware, we can split that into two problems. One is motion, which means we want the hardware to physically move and respond in ways the models can predict. The other is sensing: we want the hardware platform to have reliable feedback that reflects the actual physical state in the real world, and that the model can learn from. [seg 2877–2885]

For example, the joint actuator transmission choice has significant effects on both motion and sensing. So I will use that as an example to explain how we use these design principles in hardware development. [seg 2886–2889]

## Motion: planetary vs. harmonic transmission

On the motion side: we just released our DYNA 2.1, and if you have seen our demo, you probably already noticed that we use planetary actuators over harmonic. The rationale has two parts. One is that we want to minimize the internal resistance — the transmission loss. The other is that we want a simpler, more straightforward actuator model that the models can adapt to. [seg 2890–2896]

If you look at the left side of the plot, this is a published result from previous researchers: given the same motor and similar gear ratios, harmonic drives have approximately two times the internal resistance of a planetary gearbox. [seg 2897–2899]

The second part is the actuator model. For harmonic drives, the modeling demands are more complex; we require a more detailed and complex model to reflect the performance of harmonic drives. Here are two examples. One is hysteresis: as we know, there are soft gears inside the harmonic drive, and the deformation depends not only on the current torque input but also on previous loadings, which makes modeling the harmonic drive quite complex. The second is that the internal resistance changes with dynamic loading conditions and temperature conditions. Altogether, this makes modeling a harmonic joint actuator not impossible, but much more complex than a simpler planetary transmission. So, based on that, our rationale is to reduce the resistance and simplify the actuator model with planetary transmissions. [seg 2900–2911]

To show one of our internal testing results: we let the robot follow a very simple circular motion with the same RL〔?〕 policy. The left one is in the simulation environment〔?〕, and the two plots below are the position tracking errors. The middle one is our robot, Taku, doing the same motion with the same RL policy. And the right one is the trajectory difference for the left and right hands. One thing I want to emphasize here is the increase in tracking root-mean-square error from simulation to hardware: from our testing results, this is only 0.3 mm. I think this is proof that with this planetary gearbox transmission, we can achieve quite good results from simulation to the real robot. [seg 2912–2929]

## Sensing: current-to-torque transparency

The second part is sensing. The biggest influence of transmission selection here is current-to-torque transparency. In general, most robot applications are contact-rich manipulation, and for contact-rich manipulation, the arms and end effectors are central to the interaction with the physical world. We also need force feedback to guide the robot's behavior. There are other ways, for example adding joint torque sensors to the robot, but the reason why we chose a different way is beyond the scope of this talk, so I will just focus on current-to-torque transparency. [seg 2930–2946]

From the bottom diagram, it's quite clear that if we want to get the external force estimation, we first need a very accurate actuator model to get the joint torque estimation. By having a simple, easier-to-model actuator transmission, we can have an accurate enough actuator model to give us a very accurate joint torque estimation, and thus a very good external force estimation. [seg 2947–2953]

To prove that, we did some simulations in-house. One of the tests applies an external force on both the wrist and the elbow of our robot: a 15 N force applied along the y-axis. From the plot, you can see that the mean absolute error for the wrist is around 1.39 N and for the elbow about 0.8 N. Just for context, these are only simulation results, but the latest literature baseline is about 1.05 N, from the ARX arm — the X5〔?〕 robotic arm from ARX — which also uses a planetary solution. [seg 2954–2966]

## Conclusion

To conclude, the way we solve hardware for fast-evolving models is to have predictable hardware for evolving intelligence. By that we mean we want the hardware to have motion that the model can predict, and we want the hardware platform to provide reliable feedback that the models can trust. In short, we want to design the hardware to narrow the sim-to-real gap, so that the hardware can last long enough for fast-evolving models. [seg 2967–2971]
