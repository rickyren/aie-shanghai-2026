## Why HeyGen builds a video agent

For those of you who don't know what HeyGen does: we are an AI video company. However, we don't make short dramas, Hollywood films, etc. Those are amazing videos, and there are many great companies like MiniMax and Seedance and many others focusing on those types of videos. At HeyGen, our mission and vision is that we believe video is one of, if not the best, communication formats. Most people would rather watch a one-minute video than read a five-page document. Internally, we have replaced a lot of our documentation with video. And we believe video is a critical tool for everyone here, builders, to communicate your ideas, your stories, and your products to the world. So we believe we need to build an agent that allows our users to make these types of videos with ease. [seg 325–338]

## The problem with a 2,000-line JSON context

When we started, we built an agent on top of the video editor that we have built over the years. This video editor has your script on the left-hand side, and you can change the colors, edit the scenes. And this editor is built on top of at least 2,000 lines of JSON — sometimes 6,000, sometimes 10,000 — because most of our products, before the agentic world, were built on things like JSON, XML, or whatever underlying data structure. So your first inclination when you build an agent is to build an agent that can write your format — in this case, this 2,000-line JSON. [seg 339–348]

But the problem with that is that most agents, unless you train your own model, aren't trained to write your JSON. They are trained to write JSON. Don't get me wrong: agents are really good at writing JSON. But agents actually cannot reason within your JSON, at least not to the extent that we would want. For instance, these are two four-line JSONs. They look perfectly fine to an agent. However, one is laid out correctly, and in the other one you can't even see the words. But when an agent is just looking at those lines, it has no idea that it made a mistake. So when we were working on context engineering, we had to feed this image back into the agent, and the agent goes, "Oh, shoot, I made a mistake. Let me change those numbers." [seg 349–361]

Internally, we had this analogy when we were building the entire video agent on top of this 2,000-line JSON context: we were trying to harness the LLM's intelligence into something it's actually not that good at. We say it's almost like asking Shakespeare to write a Chinese poem. You give him a bunch of Chinese poem examples, and he can actually write something. It looks okay; it looks like a poem. But is it a great one? Absolutely not. So we decided we needed to reshape our agent's context so that we send Shakespeare back to writing plays. [seg 362–371]

## Reshaping the context: HyperFrames

So what are agents trained on? What are they really, really good at? I think everyone would now agree that most, if not all, of the frontier labs' LLMs are incredible at writing code, and they will continue to be. So what if our entire context is built on top of code and file systems? So we threw out our JSON completely and just let agents write HTML. And that's when we started building HyperFrames. HyperFrames is an open-source framework that we open-sourced earlier this year, in April, where we let agents write pure HTML, CSS, and JavaScript code, and with the framework you can render an MP4 video out of it. [seg 372–380]

You might be thinking: "What? HTML video? What does that mean?" On the left-hand side, you see a very simple piece of HTML: a couple of divs, titles, some JavaScript. And on the right is the video that this HTML creates. So imagine a mental model: you're looking at the landing page of a website, and imagine that website just kept on changing. The words fly in, fly out, flip, animate. A video comes into the scene, goes full screen, goes back to the center. Sound comes on, etc. Sure, you're looking at a landing page, but that can also be a video. That's the core idea of this context reshaping. [seg 381–393]

When we open-sourced it, we claimed that agentic video is HTML. And 22 days later, Anthropic agreed — they copied our idea. They said, "HTML is the new markdown," "the unreasonable effectiveness of HTML," and didn't mention us at all. That's okay. [seg 394–400]

But I want to show you a real quick example of what our agent can do now. [seg 401–401]

Before we see the final output: people called our videos slop. Oh, looks like something is playing — HyperFrames. Thank you very much. This video was made by me. I have never learned After Effects, Premiere, or Final Cut 〔?〕. I don't even know how to use CapCut. But now I can make these types of videos to communicate the value of our products just by prompting our agent. I think it's an incredible unlock for me and for everyone here, because we built it for AI builders — for the builders who have a story to tell but don't know how to make a good video. [seg 418–431]

But like I said, when we first started, just four or six months ago, people thought the videos we made were slop. They were. And they still kind of are today. But models get better over time, and every single time the model gets better, our product gets better. As of today, we have about 80,000 GitHub stars. We are the number two most-installed skill globally, and I think we are hitting 700,000 videos a day; I think a week ago it was 500. [seg 432–442]

## Build on what models are good at

So this talk is not just about convincing you that HyperFrames is a great product. It is — you should be using our tools. But it's more about how we landed on building HyperFrames and the agentic system on top of it: we believe agents are incredibly good at coding. They might be good at many other things too, but just think about coding alone. Many companies, including the frontier labs, are replacing documents with HTML. Many Claude artifacts or GPT outputs are actually code. Design: one of my favorite AI tools is called Paper, and there are a few others. They've built an entire Figma, but on top of HTML — again for the same reason: agents are really good at writing code, they know what good-looking code looks like, and they know how to write it. Going back to my Shakespeare analogy: when Shakespeare is writing a play, he already knows it's a good play. He doesn't need to write the play, then read it and go, "Oh, I guess it does do that." Anyway, you get my point. [seg 443–461]

Slides: this whole slide deck you just saw is completely HTML. And many companies — Manus, Genspark, ChatGPT — are all building slides using HTML. And this is more of an extension, but gaming: games used to be built on Unity, Unreal, all these very heavy engines. But these days, many people are building games with just Three.js, JavaScript, on top of HTML. [seg 462–469]

So I said I want to convince you that HTML is all your agents need. But I do believe that thinking about what models are going to keep getting better and better at is the question you really need to be asking yourself. Will your product be riding that wave, or will you be fighting it? HyperFrames has seen this growth over the past four weeks; even if the team didn't do anything for the last four weeks, we would still grow, because our product just got better as the models got better. A very popular question in Silicon Valley these days is: when models get 10x better, does your product get better, or does it get destroyed? I think the answer to that question lies in the shape of your context. [seg 470–480]
