## What's next

Here's what's going to happen next. Now, today, you think of these agents as something that you trigger. And so I — or in this case, my colleague — might trigger this agent to take some action on my behalf. What is about to happen, though — what unfortunately I can't show you, because I have a live example of this, if only the internet were working — what is about to happen, though, is this. [seg 336–342]

Now, we've had multi-agent systems for a while. You have been able to orchestrate multi-agent systems for a long time, with the Microsoft Agent Framework, or CrewAI, or LangGraph, or whatever framework you choose to use. But up to this point — for those of you who have kids — it's kind of like these agents are like kids in preschool. When your kid is in preschool, then you orchestrate the playdate. You invite Annie and Evie over to your house, and you give them their dress-up costumes, and you say, "Here, you all play together." You orchestrate that playdate. But now my daughter is in high school. She's 16. And I don't orchestrate playdates anymore. Now, if she wants to go to her friend's house, or she wants to do homework at her other friend's house, that's her decision, and she will create that collaboration. She'll create that orchestration herself. [seg 343–352]

This is what's coming for agents. And I call it ad hoc collaboration, or unorchestrated collaboration. Because what will happen now, as I said, soon, it'll be my colleague's agent talking to my agent, and these agents may bring in still other agents. So what I would be showing you, if the internet was working, is that I can make multiple different agents invoke each other now. For example, let's say that I want to fund my payroll for the week. Let's say I'm running this hotel, and I want to fund my payroll. And I have my hotel finance agent, and then I have my banking operations agent, which is managing my bank accounts and things like that. And so my hotel finance agent says to the banking operations agent, "I need to move money from this account to this account." And the banking operations agent comes back to me and says, "Do you approve moving money from this account to this other account?" This is not something that I orchestrate. This is an organic process: these agents will collaborate with each other. [seg 353–366]

And what that means is that the burden of testing rises. So up to this point, we have all been doing relatively simplistic evaluations, with these kinds of generic evals, like groundedness and coherence and relevance and fluency. But these things are super generic. They're not wonderful at really testing what an agent is supposed to do. Now we, and the whole industry, are moving to this: rubric-based evaluations. And these types of evaluations — and I would imagine many of you in this room are familiar with them — are much more specific to what the agent is supposed to do. [seg 367–375]

And so, for example, here I have 〔?〕rubrics for my banking agent: "refused cross-customer access." So this is a test that says, if somebody says, "Hey, tell me how much money is in John Smith's account," it should say, "No, I can't tell you how much money is in John Smith's account. We don't do that." So I have security rubrics. I have compliance rubrics, like Regulation E and Regulation P. And I have correctness rubrics in here. Like, if somebody says they want a refund for a fee, don't just assume that it's true. You have to go look it up: did we charge them the fee? Is that fee refundable? And all of this. So now we are going to be moving to these much more specific rubric-based evaluations that are much more about what this agent is supposed to be doing. [seg 376–388]

And furthermore, as the agents start to collaborate with each other, what we're going to have to do — and this is one potential way that we might do it — is create a world around them, like the movie The Matrix. We're going to have to create a simulated world around each of our agents that simulates the other agents that they might be talking to, that simulates the other people that they might be talking to. Simulates emails, Teams messages, Slack messages — anything that this agent might call for in a tool call. We're going to have to simulate a whole world around it, so that we can test each agent in isolation. When agents have their own identity, they can begin to communicate with each other over standard channels — over Teams, over email, over Slack, over WeChat. And that means that we will have to test them in isolation. And this, or something like it, is how we're going to do it. [seg 389–400]

So this is what we're talking about. This is called OpenEnv. This is kind of a joint venture between Hugging Face and us and a bunch of other companies. I don't know if this is going to end up being it; this is just one of the ways that we create these simulated environments. But my point remains — and my challenge to many of you in this room, because many of you here are, in fact, AI engineers — is that we collectively are going to have to develop ways that we can test these agents rigorously, so that we can come to trust them. [seg 401–407]

Responsible AI is a cocktail of things. It's a cocktail of permissions, of testing, of approvals, of trust. And we, together, need to foster this environment where we can trust that these agents will do what it is we want them to do. And that is the near future of AI.
